APCV 2026 in Auckland: Presenting Temporal Segmentation Research

yen-ju chen July 01, 2026 November 04, 2026
news apcv visual-perception temporal-segmentation psychophysics

From July 1 to July 4, 2026, I visited Auckland, New Zealand, for APCV & EPC 2026. The meeting was held at the University of Auckland, and the trip began with a clear view of the harbour and the city before moving into several days of vision science and experimental psychology.

Joining APCV & EPC 2026

APCV, the Asia-Pacific Conference on Vision, brings together researchers working on visual perception, psychophysics, neuroscience, cognition, and computational approaches to vision. The 2026 meeting was held jointly with EPC, the Australasian Experimental Psychology Conference, which made the program especially useful for connecting basic vision research with broader questions in experimental psychology.

For PRIME Lab, the main purpose of this trip was to present our recent work on temporal segmentation and to discuss how the visual system combines different visual attributes when organizing dynamic scenes.

Poster Presentation

The poster was titled “Integration of visual attributes in spatial segmentation based on temporal asynchrony revealed by a noise masking paradigm”, with Yen-Ju Chen, Zitang Sun, and Shin’ya Nishida as authors.

Download the APCV 2026 poster

The study asks how the visual system integrates multiple attributes, such as luminance, color, and spatial phase, when segmenting a dynamic visual field. Previous work showed that temporal correlation among local elements can support spatial segmentation, but it remained unclear whether different attributes contribute through separate channels or are integrated before temporal correlation is computed.

To test this question, we used a noise masking paradigm. Participants viewed an 8 by 16 dynamic lattice made from Gaussian blobs or Gabor patches. The display could be segmented into left and right regions when elements within each region shared temporal correlation, while the two regions were uncorrelated across the boundary. In different conditions, the segmentation signal was carried by one or two attributes, and half of the elements were replaced by temporally uncorrelated noise from either the same attribute or a different attribute.

The results showed that cross-attribute noise impaired temporal segmentation, in several cases approaching the level of within-attribute noise when signal strength was matched. Luminance often produced stronger interference under full-strength conditions, but supplementary tests showed that this asymmetry depended on relative signal strength. A gain-weighted single-channel model, in which attributes are integrated before temporal correlation computation, accounted for most of the behavioral variance. These findings support the idea that temporal segmentation is not computed independently within each visual attribute; instead, signals interact through an integrated stage before segmentation decisions are made.

The poster session was a good opportunity to make this argument more explicit. Questions from other researchers focused on the boundary between attribute-specific processing and integrated temporal computation, which is exactly where this project is trying to sharpen the theory.
